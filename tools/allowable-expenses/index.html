What counts as an allowable expense

If you are a sole trader, you pay tax on your profit, not your turnover. Allowable expenses are the running costs of your business that you take away from turnover to reach that profit. A cost is allowable when it is wholly and exclusively for the business. If something is partly personal, such as a phone or a car, only the business share counts.

Common examples are stock and materials, office costs, travel, staff, premises, advertising, bank charges, and accountancy fees. Equipment such as a laptop or tools is usually deductible in full, either under the cash basis (the default for sole traders since April 2024) or through the Annual Investment Allowance. Cars are handled differently, through mileage or capital allowances.

Simplified expenses for 2026/27

Instead of working out actual costs, you can use HMRC flat rates for some expenses:

  • Cars and vans: 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles, then 25p a mile. The 55p rate applies from 6 April 2026; it was 45p before that.
  • Motorcycles: 24p a mile.
  • Working from home: £10 a month for 25 to 50 hours, £18 for 51 to 100 hours, and £26 for 101 hours or more. Phone and internet costs are claimed separately.
  • Living at your business premises: work out your total premises costs, then take off £350 a month for one person, £500 for two or £650 for three or more for private use.

Simplified expenses are for sole traders and partnerships without a company partner. You cannot use the mileage rate for a vehicle you have already claimed capital allowances on, and once you use it for a vehicle you must keep using it for that vehicle.

Expenses or the £1,000 trading allowance

You can deduct the £1,000 trading allowance instead of your actual expenses, but not both. The calculator compares the two and uses whichever gives the lower profit. If your turnover is £1,000 or less, the allowance covers it in full and there is no tax to pay.

What the result shows

The result lists each expense, the total, your taxable profit and an estimate of the Income Tax and Class 4 National Insurance on that profit. It also shows how much tax claiming the expenses saves, compared with claiming nothing.
